# Writing Your Own Feed Rules

Most social networks decide what you see. An algorithm built by a company for its own goals chooses which posts appear in your feed, in what order, and how often. You have no say in it. You cannot say show me more of this kind of thing or never show me that kind of thing. The algorithm knows best, and the algorithm works for its shareholders, not for you.

Threadlight gives you that power back. You can write a small program, called a plugin, that decides how your feed works. You can install plugins written by other people. You can share your own. The feed becomes yours.

A plugin is a small program that receives a list of posts and returns a decision about each one. There are two kinds of decisions it can make. It can rank posts by assigning a score to each one, so that higher-scoring posts appear higher in your feed. Or it can filter posts by deciding whether to keep or hide each one, so that hidden posts never reach your feed. You can do either one or both. A rank plugin might boost posts from people you trust. A filter plugin might hide posts about topics you are tired of. A hybrid plugin does both in one pass. The plugin runs on your server, not in your browser. You upload it once, and it runs every time your feed refreshes. It does not send your data anywhere — it only sees the posts that are already in your feed queue.

Plugins run inside a sandbox, which is a protected environment that gives the plugin exactly what it needs and nothing more. The sandbox has no access to the network, so a plugin cannot call out to the internet, send data to another server, or download anything. It has no access to the filesystem, so it cannot read your files or write anything to disk. It cannot access any other user's data. It receives only the posts you give it and returns only scores or decisions. Each plugin has a strict time limit of ten milliseconds. If a plugin takes longer, it is stopped automatically to prevent a slow or broken plugin from delaying your feed. The memory limit is one megabyte. If a plugin tries to use more, it is stopped. These limits mean plugins cannot do harm, not intentionally and not accidentally. They can only process the list of posts you gave them and return results.

There are three kinds of plugins you can write, and the difference is in what they return. Rank plugins are the simplest. They receive a list of posts and return a score for each one. The scores can be any number. A score of ten means show this prominently, while a score of negative five means bury this. The feed sorts posts by score, highest first. A rank plugin might give a bonus to posts from people you have high affinity with, or penalize posts that use certain words, or boost posts that are getting a lot of positive reactions. The logic is entirely yours.

Filter plugins do not score posts. They decide which ones to keep and return a list of the posts that should stay. Everything else is removed. A filter plugin might hide posts about a topic you are taking a break from, or hide posts from accounts that match a pattern you find harmful. Filtering happens before ranking. If you use both a filter plugin and a rank plugin, the filter plugin runs first to remove unwanted posts, and then the rank plugin scores the survivors.

Hybrid plugins do both in one go. They receive all posts, filter some out, and assign scores to the rest. This is useful when your filtering and ranking logic are connected. For example, you might want to penalize certain posts instead of hiding them entirely, or your scoring might depend on something you learned during filtering. Writing one plugin that does everything saves a little work and lets your logic stay together.

Writing a plugin is not as hard as it sounds. Plugins are compiled to WebAssembly, a format that runs safely on any server. You can write them in Go, Rust, or any language that compiles to WebAssembly. A plugin must export at least one of three functions: one for ranking posts, one for filtering posts, and one that tells the server your plugin's name, version, and description. The ranking and filtering functions do the actual work. The input to your plugin is always a list of posts in JSON format, and each post includes its title, body text, author, tags, community, mood, and reaction counts. Your plugin reads this list, applies its logic, and returns either a list of scores for ranking or a list of kept post IDs for filtering. The idea is simple: look at each post, decide what to do with it, and return your decision. The real code comes out to about twenty lines in any language.

When you are ready to share your plugin, you upload it through the plugins page. You give it a name, a description, a version number, and the compiled WebAssembly file. The file must be under one megabyte. After uploading, your plugin is not immediately public. An admin or trusted reviewer checks it first. The review is not about whether your plugin is good or useful — it is about whether it is safe. Does it stay within the sandbox limits? Does it do what it claims? Does it have any obvious bugs that could crash the server? The review process is transparent, and once approved, your plugin appears in the marketplace where anyone can install it, use it, and leave a review. If people find it useful, it gets more installs and higher ratings. If it has problems, reviewers and users can flag them. You can also update your plugin by uploading a new version, and anyone who installed the old version gets notified so they can upgrade or stay on the old version. You cannot push updates silently — the user always chooses.

Installing someone else's plugin is even easier. You browse the marketplace, read the description, check the rating and reviews, and when you find a plugin that sounds useful you click install. It starts working immediately on your feed. You can install as many plugins as you want, and they run in order: first all filter plugins, then all rank plugins. The order matters, so you can rearrange them. You can uninstall a plugin at any time, and your feed goes back to how it was before. No data is lost and no harm is done. The marketplace is not a store. There is no payment required, no subscription, and no tracking. Plugins on Threadlight are free, because the purpose is not to sell you something — it is to give you control over your own experience.
