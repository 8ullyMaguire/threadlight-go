# Moderation and Safety

Threadlight believes that the people who use a platform should be the ones who take care of it. Instead of leaving all the decisions to a small group at the top, everyone shares the responsibility of keeping the space healthy and safe. This is not about control by the few — it is about shared power, transparency, and community ownership.

The rules of Threadlight are short on purpose, because rules that everyone can remember are rules that everyone can follow. The first rule is that you must not deny that another person is a human being. You do not have to like someone or agree with their politics, but you must treat them as a person. No dehumanizing language, no advocating violence against any group, no organizing harassment against anyone. The second rule is that you must not lie about things that cause real harm. Opinions and interpretations are fine — everyone sees the world from their own angle — but denying observable reality when that denial causes concrete harm to people is not allowed. The third rule is simple: do not fake being a real person. Every account should belong to a real human being communicating in good faith. Bots, bought engagement, coordinated fake accounts, and manufactured popularity have no place here because they trick people and undermine trust. The fourth rule is that you must not post illegal content — whatever is against the law where the server lives is against the rules here too.

The first tier of moderation is called M1, and it is done by regular users — the people themselves. Anyone at the Regular trust level or above can rate a post with a label. Some labels are helpful, like Insightful, Interesting, Informative, Funny, or Underrated. Others are corrective, like Misleading, Off-Topic, Troll, or Flamebait. Labeling a post does not delete it or hide it from the world. It adds context, which is more powerful than censorship. A post rated Misleading by enough people will be visually marked and shown less in feeds, while a post rated Insightful will reach more people. The community decides what deserves attention and what does not. Each moderator gets a limited number of actions per day, usually five to ten. This prevents any single person from having too much influence. Unused actions expire at the end of the day, so moderation is about daily participation, not hoarding power for a rainy day. If you see something that clearly breaks the rules and you are not yet a moderator, you can report it. Reports go into a queue where moderators can review them.

The second tier of moderation is called M2, and it moderates the moderators themselves. From time to time, moderators are asked to review a decision made by another moderator. They see the post and the label that was applied, and they answer one simple question: was this moderation fair? They answer yes or no. If a moderator consistently makes decisions that others find unfair, they lose their moderation privileges. If they are consistently fair, they may earn a slightly larger daily budget because the community trusts their judgment more. There is no appeals process for individual posts — once a label is applied, it stays. This prevents moderation from turning into a game where people argue their way out of consequences. Instead, moderators are careful because their own reputation is on the line. The system is transparent and anti-hierarchical: everyone who moderates is also moderated by someone else, and no one sits above the system.

For very serious cases, like real-world threats, organized harassment, or illegal content, server administrators have direct tools. They can issue warnings, suspend accounts, or permanently ban users. But every single admin action is recorded in a public moderation log. Anyone can see what action was taken and why. This keeps the administrators accountable to the community, not the other way around. The power to remove someone is paired with the transparency of having that decision visible to all.

You also have full control over your own experience. You can block someone, which means they cannot see your posts or interact with you. You can mute someone, which works the same way but is temporary and invisible to the person you muted — they never know you did it. You can set up content filters to hide posts containing certain words or topics. And you can adjust your privacy settings to control who can message you and who can see your profile. These tools put the power of safety in your own hands, because no one knows your boundaries better than you do.

If you are being harassed, the first thing to do is block the person immediately. Then report the harassing content using the report button. Do not respond — replying often makes things worse and gives the harasser what they want. If the harassment continues through other means, contact an administrator. Harassment violates the first rule, and repeat offenders face consequences that are recorded in the public moderation log for everyone to see. Threadlight is built on the principle that safety is a shared responsibility, not a privilege granted by the few to the many. Everyone who uses this place also helps protect it.
