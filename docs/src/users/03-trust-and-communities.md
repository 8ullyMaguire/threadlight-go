# Trust and Communities

On most social networks, the loudest voices get heard the most. The more followers you have, the more attention you get, whether you know what you are talking about or not. Threadlight works differently. Instead of counting followers, it builds trust — real trust that grows over time and means something because it is rooted in shared experience, not popularity.

When you first arrive on Threadlight, you are like someone visiting a new town. You can look around, read what people are saying, follow interesting folks, and explore. This beginning phase keeps bots and troublemakers from causing trouble right away, but more importantly it gives you time to learn the culture before jumping in. After a few days of being here and taking part, you become a Basic User. Now you can create posts and reply to others without any limits — you are part of the conversation.

After a couple of weeks of steady participation, you become a Regular. This is where things get more interesting. You can flag content that breaks the rules, helping keep the platform clean for everyone. You can vote on tags, helping decide what labels best describe each post. And your trust starts to carry more weight in the system, because you have proven that you care about the community, not just about yourself.

After about a month of being a good community member, you become a Leader. Leaders can vouch for new users — a public act of trust that puts your own name on the line for someone else. Leaders also help moderate content and tags, guiding the community in a positive direction. Leaders are not bosses; they are people who have shown they can be trusted with more responsibility.

After months of active, positive participation with no serious issues, you become an Elder. Elders carry the highest trust weight and help with the most sensitive moderation cases. Their judgment matters most because they have been with the community the longest and have proven their commitment over and over. But none of this happens by grinding for points or chasing badges. Trust comes from time spent, from positive interactions where people react to your posts and follow you, from keeping a clean record, and from consistency — showing up day after day rather than bursting in and going silent. The whole idea is that trust means something real. When someone says I trust this person, it carries weight because they have proven themselves over time before the eyes of the community.

The web of trust is one of Threadlight's most powerful features. When you reach Leader level, you can vouch for other users. Vouching is a public act — your name goes on the trust connection. If someone you vouched for repeatedly breaks the rules, it affects your trust too. This is how Threadlight defends itself against bots and fake accounts. You cannot create thousands of fake profiles because you cannot find thousands of real people willing to put their own reputation on the line for them. Your trust vote also carries more weight the higher your own level is, so the judgment of someone who has been here for months matters more than a group of brand-new accounts working together. Power is distributed according to demonstrated commitment, not wealth or fame.

Communities are groups of people who share a common interest. Each community has its own rules, its own moderators, and its own culture. You can browse the list of public communities to find one that feels right. Each listing shows the name, a description, how many members it has, and whether it is open to everyone or by invitation only. When you join a new community, your votes there carry less weight for a little while. This probation period gives you time to absorb the community's ways before your actions have full effect. The longer you have been a member, the more your vote counts — growing slowly at first, then faster as you become a more established part of the group.

Each community has curators who make sure posts stay on topic. Curators can remove posts that do not belong, but they cannot ban users. Bans are handled by the broader moderation system, keeping the power to remove someone separate from the power to keep things organized. This separation of powers prevents any one person from having too much control. Any Regular user or above can create a community. New communities start in slow boot mode, which means they grow slowly at first so the founders have time to shape the culture before too many people arrive. And if a community drifts away from what it was meant to be, anyone can fork it — creating a copy of the member list and rules to start fresh in a new direction, while the original community continues as it was. This means no community is trapped under a direction the members do not agree with.

Circles are another way to discover people. Unlike communities, circles are not places you join. They are suggestions from the system — groups of people near you who share your interests. You can browse circles, find interesting people, follow them, and move on. Circles use something called location hashing, which lets you find people nearby without revealing your exact address. It is discovery without surveillance, connection without exposure.

Tag voting ties everything together. Since anyone can add a tag to any post, the community votes on which tags are the most accurate. Tags can be upvoted or downvoted. Widely upvoted tags are displayed prominently, while downvoted tags fade away. This means no single person can force a bad tag on a post and no author can prevent a truthful one from being added. People who consistently help with tags earn trust points, because tagging is a way of contributing to the shared understanding of the community — it is work done for the common good.
