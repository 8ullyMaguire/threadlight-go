# User Lists: Organizing Together

On most social networks, you are alone. You choose who to follow, who to block, and what to see. If a wave of harassment hits your community, each person has to defend themselves one by one. If someone has spent years curating a list of excellent scientists to follow, you cannot benefit from their work. You start from zero, just like everyone else.

User lists change that. They turn individual actions into shared tools, and they are one of the simplest and most powerful ideas on Threadlight. A user list is just a collection of accounts with a name and a description. You might call one climate scientists or known spammers on our instance or thoughtful writers about parenting. The name is for you and for anyone you share it with.

Every list has a type that says what happens when someone uses it. A follow list contains accounts worth following. When someone subscribes to your follow list, the system follows every person on it for them automatically. A block list contains accounts that should be blocked. When someone subscribes, everyone on the list gets blocked for them right away. This is the difference between a recommendation and a protection. Follow lists help people find good voices to listen to. Block lists help people avoid harm without having to discover every bad actor on their own.

Every list also has a visibility that says who can see it. A private list is just for you, good for your own personal organization. A public list is visible to anyone, good for sharing your curation with the whole community. A shared list is visible to people you invite, and those people can help you edit it, good for building something together with people you trust.

Creating a list is straightforward. You go to your lists page and click the button to create one. You give it a name that actually tells people what it is for. bad actors is clear. my list is not. You choose the type and the visibility. Then you start adding accounts, one at a time, by username. There is no limit to how many accounts a list can hold, though a list with thousands of members becomes hard to manage. You can also delete a list when you are done with it, and when you do, all the memberships and subscriptions go away. Anyone who subscribed will no longer be following or blocking through that list.

When you find a public list that interests you, you can subscribe to it. Subscribing applies the list's action to every account on it all at once. If the list grows later because someone adds new accounts, those new accounts are automatically followed or blocked for you too. You do not need to keep checking back. You stay subscribed even if the list changes hands or becomes collaborative. And you can unsubscribe at any time, which reverses the action for accounts that were added after you subscribed, while leaving alone any accounts you already had on your personal list. This makes lists incredibly useful for ongoing protection. Subscribe once to a well-maintained block list, and you are shielded from new bad actors without having to watch for them yourself.

Some lists are built by more than one person. A collaborative list is one where the work of maintaining it is shared. The person who created it is the owner, and the owner can invite others as editors or viewers. Editors can add and remove accounts from the list, but they cannot change its name, type, or visibility, and they cannot delete it. They are trusted curators who share the workload. Viewers can see the list and its members but cannot change anything, which is useful for transparency. A community might want everyone to see who is on a block list and why, even if only a few people maintain it. Collaboration turns a single person's effort into a community resource. A group of moderators from different communities can maintain a shared block list together. A collective of science communicators can curate a follow list of accurate voices. The work is distributed, and everyone benefits.

There are even lists that build themselves. An algorithmic list fills itself based on rules instead of manual work. You tell the system what kinds of accounts you want, and it finds them. You might set a minimum trust level so only accounts above a certain point are included. You might say an account needs to have posted recently or needs to have a certain number of positive reactions. You might limit negative reactions or require a certain number of posts on a specific topic. The system checks these rules every thirty minutes and updates the list automatically. You could create an algorithmic follow list called top rust contributors this month that looks for accounts with lots of recent posts about the Rust programming language, high positive reaction rates, and a decent trust level, and the list would stay fresh all on its own. Algorithmic lists can be public and collaborative too. You could share a rising scientists list that automatically includes accounts meeting certain criteria, and let a panel of editors override the algorithm by manually adding or removing specific accounts whenever they want.
